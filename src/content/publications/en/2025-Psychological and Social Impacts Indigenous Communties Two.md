Despite a growing social inclusiveness of global policy texts, conservation practice has not yet fully embraced the meaningful participation of local and Indigenous communities residing in protected areas. In this context, the Thai government approved two Royal Decrees for ecosystem conservation in November 2024 that would severely threaten the livelihoods, living prospects, and continued residence of Indigenous communities in protected areas. Protests with 10,000 participants prompted a review of the Decrees in May 2025. In response, this research report investigated the psychological and social implications of the announcement of the Royal Decrees and argues that it undermined community social capital and intrinsic engagement with everyday environmental heritage. We drew on qualitative and survey data, collected from 09/2024 to 05/2025 in two Pgaz K'Nyau and two Hmong communities in the northern Thai highlands, and comprising community consultations with 73 participants, 101 semi-structured interviews, and two survey rounds with 384 observations in total. We used qualitative thematic and descriptive statistical analysis to explore how community members experienced, interpreted, and responded to the announcement of the decrees. We found that Indigenous villagers experienced anxiety and unease, and that the decrees undermined community efforts to conserve and engage with local ecosystems. The continued uncertainty surrounding communities' future sparked a strong activist sentiment to resist the decrees. Disruptions of community social capital and everyday environmental heritage also had plausible and potentially significantly negative ecological downstream effects. We therefore call for an urgent correction of the conservation initiative to embrace inclusive and bottom-up natural resource governance.
