This article considers how objects of everyday use — textiles, tools, domestic architecture and ritual paraphernalia — mediate belonging for highland communities in northern Thailand who have experienced resettlement and cultural assimilation programmes. Based on longitudinal fieldwork with three communities, I trace how material practices have been strategically maintained, transformed or abandoned in response to state integration.

I show that belonging is negotiated through material engagements that exceed both official categories of ethnic identity and heritage discourse. The findings complicate linear narratives of cultural loss and recovery, and suggest that attention to the material dimension of social life offers an important corrective to text-centred accounts of identity in the region.
