This article examines how sacred landscapes in northern Thailand are produced and contested through ritual practice. Drawing on twenty months of ethnographic fieldwork in Chiang Mai and Lamphun provinces, I analyse three interrelated processes: the seasonal reactivation of pilgrimage routes, the renovation of temple compounds and their boundary markers, and the re-inscription of local history through commemorative festivals.

I argue that sacred landscape is better understood not as a stable property of places but as an achievement of ongoing ritual work — a claim that has significant implications for heritage management, where official frameworks frequently treat sacred sites as fixed objects to be conserved. The analysis shows how lay devotees, monastic communities and state heritage agencies hold divergent understandings of what makes a site sacred, and how these differences surface in disputes over renovation, access and ritual calendars.

The article contributes to anthropological debates on space and place, to the study of religion in mainland Southeast Asia, and to critical heritage studies. It concludes by reflecting on what a processual understanding of sacred landscape implies for the stewardship of religious heritage in a region undergoing rapid urban transformation.
