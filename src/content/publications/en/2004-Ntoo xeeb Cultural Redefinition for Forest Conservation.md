Critical constraints around natural resources in northern Thailand have resulted in government officials and local villagers setting up strategies to manage watershed areas. The government, with the Thai Royal Forestry Department (RFD), bases its policies on the assumption that villagers cannot live in forest areas, whereas villagers argue that they can live harmoniously in forests by using their local wisdom. Villagers, supported by members of local non-governmental organizations (NGOs) and intellectuals, have revived and redefined their local culture in relation to issues of watershed management. The Hmong, most of whom live in watershed areas, have chosen the ntoo xeeb, or the tong seng, an annual tree offering ritual, to represent how their local wisdom can play a role in regional efforts in natural resources management. It is my argument that choosing and redefining the ntoo xeeb for natural resources conservation by the Hmong in northern Thailand is a reflection of the uniting of local villagers and grassroots movements in relation to natural resources management. It is also, critically, about the representation of Hmong ethnic identity in mainstream Thai society. The Hmong have been alleged to be destroyers of forests, due the system of shifting cultivation that they have practised for decades. By contrast, this article shows how a network of Hmong villages is applying the ntoo xeeb ceremony to natural resources management. The first such ceremony I observed took place in Mae Sa Mai, a Hmong village in Chiang Mai province, during the Hmong New Year on 2 January 1998.
