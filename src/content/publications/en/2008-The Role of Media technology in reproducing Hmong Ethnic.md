Similar to people of other ethnic groups, the Hmong have their own myths, legends, and history. These stories were traditionally passed on to successive generations in the form of story telling in daily life and on special occasions. For instance, parents recounted these stories to their children over and over again before bedtime, men shared other stories at night at funerals, and ritual specialists performed them as songs or hymns during ceremonies. Nowadays, the Hmong are scattered all over the world and are a transnational ethnic group. Hmong children growing up in these countries gradually come to absorb their adopted country's nationalism, particularly the nation-state's myths, legends and histories, through the state's "technologies of power."' In addition, globalization has brought new, sophisticated cultures and ideologies to the Hmong. Hmong ethnic identity, hence, has been gradually reshaped and eroded by nationalism and globalization.
