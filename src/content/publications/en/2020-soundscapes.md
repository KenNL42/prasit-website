Festival sound in urban Chiang Mai — drumming, chanting, amplified announcements and the ambient noise of market stalls — is frequently treated as a nuisance by municipal authorities and middle-class residents. This article treats sound as a central medium through which temple communities claim space in the city. Based on recordings and interviews conducted across four temple festivals, I show how sonic practice articulates neighbourhood identities, negotiates relations with municipal regulation, and sustains devotional publics in an increasingly privatised urban environment. The analysis argues for attending to the auditory dimension of urban religious life and its regulation.
